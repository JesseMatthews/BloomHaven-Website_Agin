Dementia care at home

Familiar surroundings, familiar faces and a steady routine can make a real difference to someone living with dementia.

Dementia affects every person differently, and it changes over time. Staying in your own home can help because the place and the routines are known. Our role is to support that: keeping the day predictable, noticing when something is wrong, and giving relatives someone to talk to.

What this can include

  • Routines built around the person: meals, rest, activity and sleep
  • Gentle prompting and reassurance rather than correction
  • Personal care delivered patiently, at the person’s pace
  • Meals, drinks and keeping an eye on eating and drinking
  • Medication prompts or support, where this is agreed
  • Company, conversation and activities such as music, photographs and reminiscence
  • Observing and reporting changes in behaviour, mood or health to family and, with consent, other professionals
  • Information and a point of contact for relatives

Who it helps

  • People with early memory changes who want to stay independent for longer
  • People with a diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease, vascular dementia or another type of dementia
  • Families who are worried about safety at home or who are exhausted by caring

How it works

We aim for consistency: a small team who get to know the person, their history and what calms or unsettles them. Care plans include life history, likes and dislikes, and what has worked before. Family are involved as much as you wish. If someone lacks capacity to agree to their care, we follow the Mental Capacity Act 2005 and work with the people who know the person best.

Things to bear in mind

Dementia care often needs to grow with the person. We review regularly. If needs outgrow what we can safely provide at home, we will say so honestly and help you think about options.

Questions

Do you need a formal diagnosis?
No. Many people come to us before a diagnosis, or while waiting for one. We can still help with day-to-day support.
Can you support someone who gets upset or refuses help?
Refusal and distress are common in dementia and are usually a sign that something is wrong or unmet. Carers use calm, person-centred approaches and share what they notice so the plan can change.
Can I get respite for myself?
Yes. Many families use us for regular short breaks or overnight cover so that they can rest.
